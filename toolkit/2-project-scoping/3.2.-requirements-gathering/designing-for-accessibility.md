# Designing for accessibility

"Accessibility by Design" is the principle that products, services, and digital experiences should be built to be usable by people with the widest possible range of abilities.&#x20;

It is also helpful to remember that universal design benefits everyone. Features built for people with disabilities often improve usability for everyone. This is sometimes called the "curb-cut effect," named after curb cuts (the sloped sidewalk edges) originally built for wheelchair users, which also help parents with strollers, delivery workers with hand trucks, and travelers with rolling luggage.

Disability also isn't a fixed, small category. It's a spectrum that includes permanent disabilities (blindness, deafness), temporary ones (a broken arm, an ear infection), and situational ones (holding a baby in one arm, being in a loud environment, using a phone in bright sunlight). Designing for accessibility means designing for this whole spectrum.

{% hint style="info" %}
**Remember to understand the dynamics of disability and context :** many disabled people do not ask for their disability to be “taken away”, but for our products, tools, social spaces and infrastructure to be made with and for them. And just like any other design practices we are accounting for history, culture, and context that lead to discriminatory outputs in the first place. Approaches to ‘who is disabled or not’ is often measured along lines of power. That is who is considered as a default human versus who has to constantly reiterate their humanity.
{% endhint %}

It should be integrated from the very start of the design process, rather than retrofitted afterward as an accommodation or compliance fix. This includes technical and user interaction, visual design requirements and social parameters of accessibility.&#x20;

#### Some key reflection questions:&#x20;

* **Have you engaged or worked with users and beneficiaries of your product or service who are disabled?**&#x20;

Don't assume that everyone has access to assistive devices to use your product. For example, a lot of assistive devices tend to be very expensive, and a poor disabled person may be hindered from accessing the full functionality of your product. Likewise, accessing disabled users can also potentially product design and development towards even more positive impact, use and reach of the product. For example, some disabled people may find that when cities have a delivery option for their monthly medical supplies, it allows them access their needed medication without having to bear the cost and risks of mobility.

{% hint style="info" %}
When you think of designing for disability, what’s the first thing that comes to mind? Maybe it’s a reserved parking spot, a ramp up to an entryway. Designing digital products and services need to account for this diversity of usage as well.
{% endhint %}



* **How would a poor disabled person use your product or access the benefit of your design?**

Design here could support them accessing your product fully. Consider physical, virtual, service or communal spaces. Let's take a citizen portal that requires the user to prove that they are human for example. Have you created or enabled an audio option for CAPTCHA? Have you considered other approaches to “prove” a person is human?





*   **In terms of graphic design, how have you included the following sensory considerations:**&#x20;

    * Hypersensitivity to light and sound or cannot hear at all?
    * Colourblindness?&#x20;
    * Those who are partially sighted or totally blind?
    * Those who cannot read in certain fonts? &#x20;


* **How accessible is your content on a cognitive level?**&#x20;

This often relate to how you present content in an easily perceivable and understandable manner.\
Can the reader skim through your content? Is it easily understandable by people at various knowledge levels? Is it less technical? Make it short. Avoid big blocks of text, where possible. <br>
